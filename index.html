          How it reads a photo

          Four steps, shown on the photo above. Everything runs on your device; the photo never leaves it.

          Finds the outline

          Picks the dark shape that looks like the molar and fills it in, at any angle or size and in uneven light.

          Lines it up

          Fits the photo onto the reference molar: rotation and scale from the outline, then a perspective correction from the printed lines.

          Separates the shading

          Keeps dark areas thick enough to be shading, ignores the printed lines, then follows the shading across any line it covers.

          Reads the depth

          Each point inside the tooth has a depth index: 0 at the surface, 1 at the enamel–dentin junction, 2 at the pulp.

          How it reads an X-ray

          Everything below runs on your device. The optional AI second opinion is the only part that sends anything anywhere, and only when you press its button.

          Works out what it is

          A white page with dark lines is the diagram; a colour photo is a cut tooth; a wide X-ray is panoramic; upper and lower crowns facing each other across a dark gap is a bite-wing; anything else is a single-tooth X-ray. You can change it.

          Whole X-rays

          On panoramic and bite-wing images it finds the bite line between the jaws, metal fillings, crowns, root fillings and implants, and on panoramic images how the head was positioned. Tap a tooth: it is numbered from the spacing of the gaps between crowns, cut out, turned crown-up if it is an upper tooth, and measured.

          One tooth

          A reference molar, premolar or front tooth is lined up with the tooth: by its silhouette on a single X-ray, by its crown's edges on a tooth cut from a whole X-ray. You can move, size, turn and stretch it by hand.

          Looks for dark areas

          Decay lets more X-rays through, so it shows darker than sound enamel and dentin. Each point of the crown is compared with the sound tissue around it; dark patches that open onto the surface or a filling are kept, while gaps between teeth, burn-out and outline mismatches are left out.

          Reads the depth

          The marked area is read with the depth index and the two thresholds fitted to your diagram dataset: past 29% of the way from the junction to the pulp is dentin, touching the pulp outline is pulp. Then the notes: which surface, fillings, burn-out, root tips, image quality.

          Limits: the reference teeth are averages, so an unusual tooth only fits roughly, and the reading is only as good as that fit. On simulated test images, single-tooth X-rays read correctly; teeth cut from a simulated panoramic X-ray were numbered correctly 31 of 31 times and read correctly 23 of 31 times, and teeth cut from a simulated bite-wing were found 8 of 8 times but read correctly only 2 of 8 times. None of it has been checked against labelled real radiographs, so check the outline and the red area every time.

          Reading decay on a radiograph

          What a dental radiology atlas teaches about finding decay on X-rays, and how X-ray mode puts each point to use.

          Use the right film

          Bite-wings are the key radiograph for early decay between back teeth, because the beam passes straight through the contact areas. Periapical films show the root tips and the bone around them, but decay between teeth is easy to miss on them.

          X-ray mode names the surface a lesion is on and which film shows it best.

          What can show at all

          Decay shows only where the beam grazes the surface and no sound tooth lies over it. Decay between teeth shows early; fissure decay on the biting surface only once it is well into dentin; decay on the cheek or tongue side hardly ever. Lesions also reach further than they look.

          Occlusal readings say the lesion is probably bigger than it looks.

          Cervical burn-out

          The neck of the tooth, between the enamel and the bone, is covered by neither, so X-rays pass easily and it shows dark on most radiographs. It can pass for root decay, and next to a metal filling it can fake an overhang. Burn-out darkens both sides of the neck alike.

          Dark bands at both sides of the neck are reported as burn-out and left out of Find dark areas.

          Fillings and secondary decay

          Metal fillings, crowns and root fillings stop X-rays and show bright white. A dark area along a filling's edge can be recurrent decay, but some liners and tooth-coloured fillings let X-rays through and look dark as well.

          Very bright areas are kept out of the "sound tooth" brightness, and dark areas against them are flagged as possible secondary decay.

          Beyond the crown

          Decay into the pulp can lead to apical periodontitis. Early on the radiograph shows nothing; acute inflammation gives a diffuse dark area with a widened ligament space; a long-standing lesion is sharply bordered with a bright rim. An intact lamina dura is a sign of health, and no radiograph shows whether the pulp is alive.

          When the root tips are in view, a dark halo around one is flagged as possible apical periodontitis.

          Image quality

          The best radiograph has the most contrast. A grey or light film, from under-exposure or under-development, makes lesions look smaller and hides early ones; a dark, fogged film exaggerates burn-out. A bent film or a wrongly angled beam stretches or shortens the tooth.

          Low-contrast, light or dark images, and a tooth that had to be stretched a lot to fit, are flagged.

          Summarised in our own words from F. A. Pasler, Color Atlas of Dental Medicine: Radiology (Thieme): the chapters on bite-wing radiographs, radiographic anatomy, errors in technique and processing, regressive changes, apical periodontitis and filling materials.

          Learn to read dental X-rays

          Short notes on what each X-ray shows, what can fool you on a panoramic image, and what else turns up besides decay. Tap a topic to open it.

          Which X-ray shows what
          • Bite-wing. The X-ray for decay between back teeth: the beam passes straight through the contacts, so early lesions show. It also shows the edges of fillings and crowns, calculus, and the level of the bone between teeth. It does not show the root tips.
          • Periapical. One or a few teeth from crown to root tip, with the bone around the tip. The X-ray for lesions at the root tips and for root canal work. Decay between teeth is easy to miss or underrate on it.
          • Panoramic (OPG). All teeth, both jaws, the sinuses and the jaw joints in one low-dose image: the usual first X-ray for a new patient, and good for impacted or missing teeth, cysts and the bone level. Not reliable for early decay, and sizes vary across the image, so it is not for precise measuring.
          • Occlusal. A view from above or below the jaw: for finding where something lies (an extra tooth, a salivary stone, the spread of a cyst).
          • 3D imaging (CT). When the third dimension matters: the exact position of an impacted tooth, a large cyst, the sinus.
          How a panoramic X-ray is made, and why it distorts
          • Only a curved slab of the face, a few millimetres thick and shaped like the dental arch, is sharp. Anything in front of it or behind it is blurred, and shown too narrow or too wide.
          • Front teeth that sit in front of the sharp layer look narrow and blurred; teeth behind it look wide and blurred.
          • Magnification is not the same everywhere, so measurements on a panoramic image are only approximate.
          • Structures outside the sharp layer, such as the other side of the jaw or the spine, are projected over the teeth and bone and can look like disease.
          • It is read in four regions: the teeth and the bone around them; the upper jaw with the nose and sinuses; the lower jaw; the jaw joints with the area behind the upper jaw and the neck.
          Positioning mistakes on panoramic X-rays
          • Chin tipped down. The bite line curves up steeply like a big smile, the upper premolars overlap, and the jaw joints sit high. The app flags a deep curve.
          • Chin tipped up. The bite line is flat or curves the wrong way, the hard palate lies across the roots of the upper teeth, and the jaw joints are pushed out to the sides. The app flags a flat curve.
          • Head turned or tilted. Teeth on one side look wider than on the other, and left and right can no longer be compared. The app flags a bite line that climbs to one side.
          • Tongue not against the palate. The air above the tongue shows as a dark band over the roots of the upper teeth, which can vanish in it.
          • Front teeth outside the sharp layer. They come out blurred and too wide or too narrow.
          • Moving during the exposure. Steps or waves in the lower border of the jaw.
          • Earrings, glasses, piercings, a lead apron. Besides the real image, a blurred, enlarged ghost appears on the other side and higher up.
          Normal anatomy that can look like disease
          • Maxillary sinus. A dark space above the upper back teeth. Its recesses and bony partitions can look like a cyst.
          • Mental foramen. A round dark spot near the roots of the lower premolars that looks like a lesion at a root tip. An intact ligament space and lamina dura around the root tip argue against a lesion.
          • Mandibular canal. A dark band edged by thin bright lines, running through the lower jaw below the roots.
          • Thin bone at the chin and under the molars. Hollows on the inner side of the jaw let X-rays through and can be taken for cysts.
          • Soft tissue and air. The tongue, soft palate, lips and cheeks add brightness; air in the throat and nose subtracts it. Neither means the bone is denser or thinner.
          • Things projected over the jaw. The hyoid bone over the lower jaw, the spine over the chin, the styloid process, the coronoid process over the back of the upper jaw, the incisive canal between the upper central incisors.
          • At the tooth. Cervical burn-out at the neck of the tooth, overlapping contacts between teeth, and the pulp horns can all look like decay.
          At the root tips: lesions and cysts
          • Apical periodontitis. Often follows decay into the pulp. Early on the X-ray shows nothing; acute inflammation gives a diffuse dark area and a widened ligament space; a long-standing lesion has a sharp border, often with a bright rim of reactive bone.
          • Radicular cyst. A round dark area with a bright, sharp border, the root tip of a dead tooth inside it, and neighbouring structures pushed aside. An infected cyst loses its sharp border. A small cyst cannot be told from a granuloma on an X-ray.
          • Residual cyst. The same, where a tooth has been taken out.
          • Follicular cyst. Around the crown of a tooth that has not come through.
          • Keratocyst. Often with several chambers, most often near the angle of the lower jaw or the canines.
          • Some tumours, such as ameloblastoma, can look just like a cyst, so tissue removed from a cyst-like lesion is examined under the microscope.
          Bright spots that are not in the jaw
          • Calculus (tartar). Small bright spurs on the sides of the teeth near the gum.
          • Salivary stones. In the duct under the tongue they show near the angle of the jaw or over the premolar and molar roots, where they can pass for a root fragment.
          • Phleboliths and calcified lymph nodes. Round, sometimes ringed, bright spots in the cheek or neck.
          • Ossified stylohyoid ligament. A long thin bright line behind the jaw.
          • An X-ray is flat, so a second view at another angle is needed to tell whether a bright spot is in the bone or in the soft tissue in front of or behind it.
          Unusual teeth
          • Extra teeth. A mesiodens between the upper central incisors; extra premolars or fourth molars.
          • Impacted teeth. Third molars and canines most often; they can cause pain and cysts for years unnoticed.
          • Ankylosis. A root fused to the bone, with no ligament space around it.
          • Shape. Taurodontism (a tall pulp chamber, short roots), dens in dente, enamel pearls near the neck, hypercementosis (bulbous roots, often on dead teeth).
          • Inherited. Amelogenesis imperfecta (thin or defective enamel); dentinogenesis imperfecta (bulbous crowns, short roots, pulps that disappear).
          Teeth and the sinus
          • Inflammation at the root tips or around the upper back teeth can spread to the floor of the sinus. Sinus disease from a tooth is usually on one side; from the nose, usually on both.
          • A panoramic X-ray shows only the lower part of the sinus well: a thickened lining or a dome-shaped retention cyst on its floor.
          • A panoramic X-ray taken with the chin up can make a healthy sinus look cloudy.
          Photographing an X-ray with your phone
          • The X-ray file itself is always better than a photo of it: ask the practice to send it, or save it from the viewer.
          • On a screen: turn the brightness up, turn the room lights down, and hold the phone straight on so the image is not a trapezium. Step back a little and zoom if you see wavy patterns.
          • On film: put it on a light box or a bright window, cover the light around the film, and avoid reflections.
          • No flash. Fill the frame with the X-ray, then use Adjust image to crop away the rest and boost the contrast.

          Print it, shade it, photograph it

          Your original printed diagram works, and so does this copy of it. Shade one lesion per tooth, then use Take a photo.

          • Shade with a dark pen, marker or soft pencil; very light shading can be missed.
          • Keep the whole tooth in the frame. Any rotation is fine, even upside down.
          • Shoot from above in even light, without a hard shadow across the tooth.
          • Touching the pulp outline counts as pulp involvement, so stop short of it if you mean dentin.
