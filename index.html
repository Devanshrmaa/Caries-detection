          How it reads a photo

          Four steps, shown on the photo above. Everything runs on your device; the photo never leaves it.

          Finds the outline

          Picks the dark shape that looks like the molar and fills it in, at any angle or size and in uneven light.

          Lines it up

          Fits the photo onto the reference molar: rotation and scale from the outline, then a perspective correction from the printed lines.

          Separates the shading

          Keeps dark areas thick enough to be shading, ignores the printed lines, then follows the shading across any line it covers.

          Reads the depth

          Each point inside the tooth has a depth index: 0 at the surface, 1 at the enamel–dentin junction, 2 at the pulp.

          Reading an X-ray or a cut tooth

          The same depth scale, applied to a real image. You line the tooth up with the reference molar; everything after that runs on your device.

          Fits the outline

          Auto-fit finds the brightest tooth-shaped blob near the middle of the image and fits the reference molar to its silhouette, in any rotation and either way round. You can move, size, turn and stretch it by hand.

          Looks for dark areas

          Decay lets more X-rays through, so it shows darker than sound enamel and dentin. The app compares each point of the crown with the sound tissue around it and keeps dark patches that open onto the tooth surface.

          Reads the depth

          The marked area is read with the same depth index and the same two thresholds fitted to your diagram dataset: past 29% of the way from the junction to the pulp is dentin, touching the pulp outline is pulp.

          Limits: the reference is one molar, so a tooth with a different shape only fits roughly, and the depth reading is only as good as that fit. Overlapping teeth, tooth-coloured fillings and the pulp horns can still look like decay; burn-out and metal fillings are recognised, but not perfectly. The X-ray reading has not been checked against labelled radiographs.

          Reading decay on a radiograph

          What a dental radiology atlas teaches about finding decay on X-rays, and how X-ray mode puts each point to use.

          Use the right film

          Bite-wings are the key radiograph for early decay between back teeth, because the beam passes straight through the contact areas. Periapical films show the root tips and the bone around them, but decay between teeth is easy to miss on them.

          X-ray mode names the surface a lesion is on and which film shows it best.

          What can show at all

          Decay shows only where the beam grazes the surface and no sound tooth lies over it. Decay between teeth shows early; fissure decay on the biting surface only once it is well into dentin; decay on the cheek or tongue side hardly ever. Lesions also reach further than they look.

          Occlusal readings say the lesion is probably bigger than it looks.

          Cervical burn-out

          The neck of the tooth, between the enamel and the bone, is covered by neither, so X-rays pass easily and it shows dark on most radiographs. It can pass for root decay, and next to a metal filling it can fake an overhang. Burn-out darkens both sides of the neck alike.

          Dark bands at both sides of the neck are reported as burn-out and left out of Find dark areas.

          Fillings and secondary decay

          Metal fillings, crowns and root fillings stop X-rays and show bright white. A dark area along a filling's edge can be recurrent decay, but some liners and tooth-coloured fillings let X-rays through and look dark as well.

          Very bright areas are kept out of the "sound tooth" brightness, and dark areas against them are flagged as possible secondary decay.

          Beyond the crown

          Decay into the pulp can lead to apical periodontitis. Early on the radiograph shows nothing; acute inflammation gives a diffuse dark area with a widened ligament space; a long-standing lesion is sharply bordered with a bright rim. An intact lamina dura is a sign of health, and no radiograph shows whether the pulp is alive.

          When the root tips are in view, a dark halo around one is flagged as possible apical periodontitis.

          Image quality

          The best radiograph has the most contrast. A grey or light film, from under-exposure or under-development, makes lesions look smaller and hides early ones; a dark, fogged film exaggerates burn-out. A bent film or a wrongly angled beam stretches or shortens the tooth.

          Low-contrast, light or dark images, and a tooth that had to be stretched a lot to fit, are flagged.

          Summarised in our own words from F. A. Pasler, Color Atlas of Dental Medicine: Radiology (Thieme): the chapters on bite-wing radiographs, radiographic anatomy, errors in technique and processing, regressive changes, apical periodontitis and filling materials.

          Print it, shade it, photograph it

          Your original printed diagram works, and so does this copy of it. Shade one lesion per tooth, then use Take a photo.

          • Shade with a dark pen, marker or soft pencil; very light shading can be missed.
          • Keep the whole tooth in the frame. Any rotation is fine, even upside down.
          • Shoot from above in even light, without a hard shadow across the tooth.
          • Touching the pulp outline counts as pulp involvement, so stop short of it if you mean dentin.

          A teaching aid. It does not diagnose real teeth: the X-ray mode maps a dark area onto a reference molar and has not been validated on radiographs. Treatment decisions need a dentist's clinical examination.
